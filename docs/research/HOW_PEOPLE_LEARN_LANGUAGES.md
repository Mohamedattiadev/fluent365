# How Adults Learn Languages: Evidence Summary for Fluent365

Scope: adult learners going from B1 to C1 / IELTS General Training 7.5 in about 365 days at 1–2 h/day.
Purpose: decide what the app's automatic "Start today, then Next" session contains.
Access date for every source: **2026-11-16** (written as "acc. 2026-11-16").

**How sources are marked.** **[V]** means the claim was checked against the source or a reliable secondary summary during this research. **[B]** means it is a standard bibliographic citation from the SLA literature that was not re-fetched. Treat [B] figures as well established but worth a second check before quoting them to users.

## 1. Big picture: how much time is needed

| Fact | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Cambridge's guideline guided learning hours (cumulative from zero): **B1 ≈ 350–400 h, B2 ≈ 500–600 h, C1 ≈ 700–800 h, C2 ≈ 1,000–1,200 h**. "Guided learning hours" include lessons plus set tasks, and learners vary widely. | [V] Cambridge English, *Introductory Guide to the CEFR for English Language Teachers*, guided learning hours table: https://cefr.schools.ac.cy/downloads/GuideToCEFR.pdf (Cambridge document hosted by the Cyprus Ministry). The Cambridge helpdesk says about 200 h per level: https://support.cambridgeenglish.org/hc/en-gb/articles/202838506-Guided-learning-hours (seen in a search snippet; the page returned 403 to the fetcher). acc. 2026-11-16 |
| LanguageCert, a different exam board, estimates per-level (non-cumulative) hours of 180 for B1, 180 for B2 and 200 for C1. | [V] https://www.languagecert.org/Guided-Learning-Hours acc. 2026-11-16 |

**Implication.** B1 to C1 is roughly 300–450 guided hours. 365 days × 1–2 h gives 365–730 h. **The goal is realistic only if the hours are high-quality and the learner also gets large amounts of input outside the "guided" core.** IELTS 7.5 sits around C1 (IELTS 7.0–8.0 is commonly mapped to C1).

## 2. Nation's Four Strands: the backbone

Paul Nation argues that a well-balanced course gives **roughly equal time** to four strands [V]:

1. **Meaning-focused input**: listening and reading for meaning, with about 95–98% of the words known.
2. **Meaning-focused output**: speaking and writing to communicate a message.
3. **Language-focused learning**: deliberate study of vocabulary, grammar and pronunciation, flashcards, and feedback.
4. **Fluency development**: using already-known material faster, under time pressure, with no new language.

Sources: Nation, "The Four Strands", *Innovation in Language Learning and Teaching* 1(1), 2007. Copy at https://core.ac.uk/download/357330216.pdf. Taylor & Francis chapter abstract: https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/9780203891643/chapters/10.4324/9780203891643-1. Both acc. 2026-11-16 [V via search summaries]. Nation recommends equal time across strands for self-study too ("What do you need to know to learn a foreign language?", VUW 2014).

**Implication for apps.** Most apps over-weight strand 3 (drills) and under-weight strands 1 and 4. Language-focused learning should be **about 25% of total time, and never more than about 35%**.

## 3. Input: comprehensible input, extensive reading and listening

- **Krashen's Input Hypothesis**: acquisition happens mainly through understanding messages slightly beyond the current level ("i+1"). This is influential but criticised as hard to falsify. [B] Krashen, *Principles and Practice in SLA* (1982): http://www.sdkrashen.com/content/books/principles_and_practice.pdf
- **Coverage thresholds.** Laufer (1989) put the minimum at **95%** known words. Hu & Nation (2000) and Schmitt et al. (2011) recommend **98%** for adequate unassisted reading comprehension. Schmitt et al. found a roughly *linear* relationship between coverage and comprehension rather than a sharp threshold. [V] Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe 2011: https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf acc. 2026-11-16
- **Extensive Reading Foundation (ERF)**: extensive reading means reading "quickly and enjoyably with adequate comprehension … without a dictionary", usually with graded readers. At **98% or more known words**, a text is at the learner's "independent level". [V via secondary citations of ERF Guide 2011] https://erfoundation.org/wordpress/?p=111 , ER Toolkit https://erfoundation.org/wordpress/useful-resources/ertoolkit/ acc. 2026-11-16
- **Incidental learning from input works but is slow.** A meta-analysis by Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa (2023, *Language Teaching*) found learners pick up only about **13–17% of target words** from a single exposure condition, with similar gains for reading, listening and reading-while-listening. [V] https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C acc. 2026-11-16. Repetition matters (r ≈ .34 in a 2019 meta-analysis, same search).
- **Real-world evidence for massive input.** Countries that **subtitle** TV rather than dub it (Nordics, Netherlands) show much higher English proficiency, especially listening and speaking. [V] Baumeister, Hanushek & Wößmann, IZA DP 17991: https://docs.iza.org/dp17991.pdf acc. 2026-11-16. Dutch children who watched English TV with subtitles learned more vocabulary (Koolstra & Beentjes 1999) [V via search summary].

**Implication.** Every session needs **substantial, level-appropriate input**: graded or adapted texts and audio at 95–98% coverage, with light support (tap for gloss) rather than a dictionary hunt. Intentional vocabulary study is needed *alongside* input because incidental uptake per exposure is low.

## 4. Output and Noticing

- **Output Hypothesis (Swain 1985, 1995).** Producing language pushes learners to (a) **notice gaps** between what they want to say and what they can say, (b) **test hypotheses** about the language, and (c) **reflect metalinguistically**. Swain also lists automaticity/fluency as a function. [V via summaries] Shehadeh, "Testing the output hypothesis", *SSLA*: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/testing-the-output-hypothesis/8437C53035A5FD15BD985463E5428B36 acc. 2026-11-16
- **Noticing Hypothesis (Schmidt 1990, *Applied Linguistics* 11(2)).** Input becomes intake only when it is consciously noticed. The strong form is debated, but weaker forms ("more noticing → more learning") are widely accepted. [V via summaries] https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/download/1428/483/3628 acc. 2026-11-16

**Implication.** Sessions should include **pushed output** (say or write something, then compare with a model) and **input enhancement**: highlighting the target collocation or grammar form inside texts.

## 5. Memory: spacing, retrieval, interleaving, desirable difficulty

- **Forgetting curve.** Ebbinghaus (1885) was replicated by Murre & Dros (2015, *PLOS ONE*): steep early forgetting, then a flattening curve. [V] https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0120644 acc. 2026-11-16
- **Spacing.** Cepeda et al. (2008, *Psychological Science*, more than 1,350 participants) found that the optimal gap between study sessions **grows with the retention interval**. For a 1-week test it is about **20–40%** of that interval, and the proportion **shrinks** as the retention interval grows (to a few weeks' gap for a 1-year test). The ratio is not constant. [V] https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf acc. 2026-11-16
- **Retrieval practice / testing effect.** In Roediger & Karpicke (2006), after **one week** the repeated-testing group recalled **about 61%** of a passage versus **about 40%** for repeated-study, even though restudying looked better at 5 minutes. [V] https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf acc. 2026-11-16
- **FSRS** (the Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler, now built into Anki) models each card's stability and difficulty. **Desired retention defaults to 0.90**, and the tutorial gives a sensible range of 0.70–0.97. Higher retention means sharply more reviews. [V] https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-optimal-retention ; tutorial https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/blob/main/docs/tutorial.md ; Anki FAQ https://faqs.ankiweb.net/what-spaced-repetition-algorithm acc. 2026-11-16. The open-source **ts-fsrs** library makes FSRS usable in a PWA [B].
- **Desirable difficulties (Bjork & Bjork 2011).** Spacing, interleaving, testing and varied conditions slow apparent progress but improve long-term retention. The authors stress that the level of difficulty must be manageable, because undesirable difficulty hurts learning. [V] https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf acc. 2026-11-16
- **Interleaving in L2 grammar.** Nakata & Suzuki (2019, *Modern Language Journal* 103(3)) studied 115 Japanese EFL learners. Mixed grammar practice improved long-term retention compared with blocked practice. Effects are clearer for grammar than for vocabulary, and interleaving works best when the categories are confusable. [V via summary] https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/dynamic-interplay-between-practice-type-and-practice-schedule-in-a-second-language/C01D682D82EDBD00AF29965FCA57BAF3 acc. 2026-11-16

**Implication.** Use an FSRS scheduler (target 0.90). Do **reviews before new items**. Make every exercise **retrieval-based** (recall before reveal, never just "read the card"). After a grammar point is introduced, **interleave** it with related points (for example, present perfect versus past simple).

## 6. Vocabulary

**Vocabulary size by CEFR level for English** (Milton & Alexiou 2009, XLex test, out of the 5,000 most frequent lemmas). These figures are indicative, not absolute requirements. [V, Table 2 in Milton & Alexiou, GALA 2010: https://www.enl.auth.gr/gala/14th/Papers/English%20papers/Milton&Alexiou.pdf acc. 2026-11-16]

| CEFR | XLex words known (of 5,000) |
|---|---|
| A2 | 1,500–2,500 |
| B1 | 2,750–3,250 |
| B2 | 3,250–3,750 |
| C1 | 3,750–4,500 |
| C2 | 4,500–5,000 |

Further evidence:
- **Nation (2006)**: 98% coverage of written text needs about **8,000–9,000 word families**, and spoken text needs about **6,000–7,000**. [V] https://openaccess.wgtn.ac.nz/articles/journal_contribution/How_large_a_vocabulary_is_needed_for_reading_and_listening_/12552221/1 acc. 2026-11-16. Van Zeeland & Schmitt (2012) suggest 95% coverage (**2,000–3,000 families**) may suffice for everyday listening: https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/32292 acc. 2026-11-16
- **IELTS link.** Milton, Wade & Hopkins (2010): orthographic vocabulary size explains **about 58% of the variance** in overall IELTS band. IELTS itself advises teaching **frequent words first** and points to the GSL, AWL and AVL lists. [V] https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/how-to-address-vocabulary-in-an-ielts-preparation-course acc. 2026-11-16
- **Lexical chunks and collocations.** Lewis (*The Lexical Approach*, 1993) argues that fluent language is built from prefabricated chunks (for example, "make a decision" or "it's worth + -ing"). IELTS band descriptors reward "less common lexical items" and "awareness of style and collocation" at band 7. [B] Lewis 1993; IELTS GT Writing band descriptors on ielts.org.
- **Incidental versus intentional learning.** Intentional study (flashcards) is fast and reliable for form–meaning links. Incidental learning from input adds depth (collocation, register, grammar patterns) but is slow (§3). Combine them: learn a word deliberately, then meet it again in input. [V, see §3]

**Targets for Fluent365.** Start at an estimated B1 (about 3,000 XLex words / about 3,000–4,000 families). Aim for about **8,000 families receptive** by the end, with a productive core of 3,000–4,000 including the AWL. That means adding roughly **12–20 new lexical items per day**, many of them chunks, which is consistent with Anki's common default of 20 new cards/day [B: https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html].

## 7. Grammar

- **Explicit instruction beats implicit on average.** Norris & Ortega (2000, 49 samples) found explicit treatments d ≈ 1.13 and implicit d ≈ 0.54. Caveat: the outcome measures often favoured explicit knowledge. [V via summaries] https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/view/1434 acc. 2026-11-16
- **Spada & Tomita (2010, *Language Learning* 60(2), 41 studies)**: explicit instruction produced larger effects than implicit for **both simple and complex** features, including on free-production measures. [V abstract, author attribution from the journal record] https://www.eas.utoronto.ca/node/790 acc. 2026-11-16
- **Focus on form (Long).** Briefly drawing attention to form *during* meaning-focused activity works better than isolated "focus on forms" alone. [B] Long 1991.
- **Processing Instruction (VanPatten).** Structured-input activities force learners to process form for meaning, for example interpreting who did what from the verb form. [B] VanPatten & Cadierno 1993.
- **Skill Acquisition Theory (DeKeyser).** Knowledge moves from declarative to procedural to automatized through **specific, meaningful practice**. Automatization is skill-specific (comprehension practice does not fully transfer to production) and follows a power-law curve, with fast early gains and slow later ones. [V] DeKeyser & Criado 2013: https://digitum.um.es/digitum/bitstream/10201/142072/1/DeKeyser%20and%20Criado_Automatization_Skill%20Acquisition%20and%20Practice%20in%20Second%20Language%20Acquisition_2013.pdf acc. 2026-11-16

**How grammar "intuition" develops.** Explicit rule, then many meaningful retrievals in both comprehension and production, then speed under time pressure. Intuition is the end product of thousands of processed instances, not of rule knowledge alone.

**Implication.** Teach each grammar point with a **short explicit explanation (about 2–3 min)**, then structured input, then controlled output, then free output. Recycle it via spaced retrieval and interleaving, and keep spotting it in reading and listening.

## 8. Corrective feedback

- **Lyster & Ranta (1997).** Teachers used recasts 55% of the time, but recasts led to the *least learner-generated repair*. Prompts (elicitation, metalinguistic clues, clarification requests, repetition) produced more self-repair. [V via secondary summaries] https://archive.carla.umn.edu/immersion/acie/vol1/Bridge1.3.pdf acc. 2026-11-16
- **Li (2010) meta-analysis** (*Language Learning* 60(2), 33 studies): a **medium overall effect** of corrective feedback that is **maintained over time**. Implicit feedback held up better at delayed tests. The commonly cited overall effect is d ≈ 0.61 [B]. [V] https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/handle/2292/24985?show=full acc. 2026-11-16

**Implication for app feedback.**
1. First wrong attempt: give a **prompt** (a hint or metalinguistic clue such as "Check the verb tense") so the learner self-corrects.
2. Second wrong attempt: give **explicit correction**, shown as a diff, plus a one-line rule.
3. Always end with the learner **producing the correct form**, by retyping it or saying it.
4. Focus on 1–3 error types per output task. Do not mark everything.

## 9. Speaking and fluency

- **4/3/2.** Give the same talk three times to new listeners in 4, then 3, then 2 minutes. Nation (1989) found speech rate rose and hesitations fell. [V via summaries] https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Fostering_fluency_in_second_language_learning ; Boers 2014 reappraisal. acc. 2026-11-16
- **Task repetition** (Bygate) improves fluency and frees attention for accuracy and complexity on later attempts. **Rehearsal** is the planning type with the most robust fluency effects. De Jong & Perfetti (2011) attribute the gains to proceduralization. [V] https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/perfettilab/pubpdfs/de%20Jong%20Language%20Learning.pdf acc. 2026-11-16
- **Shadowing.** The learner repeats audio a fraction of a second behind the speaker. It trains bottom-up processing and prosody, with evidence for listening gains (Hamada 2016, 2017) and for comprehensibility and fluency. Evidence for segmental pronunciation is weaker. [V via summaries] https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/3392195 ; https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:3104cae1-3a6b-400a-b173-de44384238d2 acc. 2026-11-16

**Implication.** In an offline PWA: record yourself, use 4/3/2 adapted to monologue with a timer and decreasing time limits, use IELTS Speaking Part 2 cue cards (1 min prep, 2 min talk) with repetition, and shadow short audio clips. Self-review against a model answer plus a checklist works where AI scoring is unavailable.

## 10. Listening

- **Field (2008, *Listening in the Language Classroom*)** criticises the "comprehension approach" (listen, then answer questions) for *testing* listening instead of *teaching* it. He recommends adding **micro-listening / decoding practice**: word segmentation, weak forms, connected speech, and rapid word recognition. Strategy training helps, but decoding is the main bottleneck for intermediate learners. [V via summaries] https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/listening-in-the-language-classroom/comprehension-approach-pluses-and-minuses/D1F8D3434837F594EC669D6CC66FA07F ; https://teachingenglishwithoxford.oup.com/2016/01/20/bottom-up-decoding-listening/ acc. 2026-11-16

**Implication.** The listening block should run: gist listen, then detail listen, then **dictation or gap-fill of 2–3 tricky sentences** (decoding), then a transcript reveal, then a shadow of the same clip.

## 11. Writing for IELTS General Training

- Format: **60 minutes**. Task 1 is a **letter of at least 150 words**, formal, semi-formal or informal. Task 2 is an **essay of at least 250 words** and counts **twice** as much as Task 1. Criteria are Task Achievement/Response, Coherence & Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range & Accuracy. [V] https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-general-training-test/ielts-general-training-format-writing acc. 2026-11-16
- Evidence-backed practice: model texts (noticing), then planning, then timed writing, then feedback against the band descriptors, then **rewriting** (output plus feedback cycle, §4 and §8).

## 12. Motivation, habit and session length

- **Habit formation.** Lally et al. (2010) found a median of **66 days** to automaticity, ranging from 18 to 254 days and varying by behaviour. **Missing one day did not materially harm** habit formation, but chronic inconsistency did. [V via summaries of *Eur. J. Soc. Psych.* 40:998] https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit acc. 2026-11-16
- **Streaks.** Duolingo reports large retention gains from streak mechanics, but this is company data. Streak anxiety can push learners toward minimum-effort "streak saving". [V company/blog-level only] https://lazyweb.com/research/duolingo-retention-experiments acc. 2026-11-16. Use **forgiving streaks**: allow 1 missed day without breaking the streak, and celebrate weekly consistency.
- **Self-Determination Theory** (Deci & Ryan; applied to L2 by Noels et al. 2000, *Language Learning* 50(1)). Motivation is sustained by **autonomy, competence and relatedness**. [B] For Fluent365 the tension is that "no decisions" lowers felt autonomy. Mitigate it with meaningful small choices that do not derail the plan (for example, a topic choice for reading) and with clear **competence feedback**: progress toward band 7.5, words known, minutes of input.
- **Cognitive load (Sweller).** Working memory is limited. Avoid introducing many new items at once, and separate new-language blocks from fluency blocks. [B] Practical consensus: blocks of **5–15 minutes** with varied modality, and a short break after about 45 minutes. Most commercial lessons run 5–30 minutes, for example Pimsleur's 30-minute audio lessons [V, below].

## 14. What learners in different countries commonly do

- **Netherlands and the Nordics.** High exposure to subtitled original-version media leads to strong listening and speaking with little formal effort [V, §3].
- **East Asia (Japan, Korea, China), and much of the Middle East and Latin America.** Exam-driven grammar-translation, vocabulary lists and cram schools are common. This produces good receptive grammar and vocabulary but weak fluency, which research attributes to too little output and fluency work (Nation's strands imbalance) [B]. Japanese ER programmes and shadowing (Kadota) emerged as fixes [V, §9].
- **IELTS candidates worldwide** (South Asia, the Middle East) rely heavily on test-format practice. That helps with familiarity, but band gains plateau without underlying vocabulary and fluency growth [B].
- **What works everywhere**: a daily habit, large comprehensible input, SRS for vocabulary, regular speaking and writing with feedback, and test practice only in the final phase.

## 15. Design rules for Fluent365

1. **Four-strands balance.** Across each week, time should split roughly 25/25/25/25 between input, output, language-focused learning and fluency. Language-focused learning (cards, grammar) is **capped at 35% of the day**.
2. **Reviews before new material, always.** A session opens with due FSRS reviews (desired retention 0.90). New items unlock only after reviews are cleared or a time cap is hit.
3. **Adaptive new-item limits.** The default is **15 new lexical items per day** (range 8–20). Auto-reduce new items when the review backlog exceeds about 1.5× a normal day or accuracy falls below 80%. Never let reviews exceed about 35% of session time. Suspend leeches after 8 lapses and re-teach them in context.
4. **Retrieval, not recognition.** Every card or exercise asks for recall or production first (type, say, or choose before reveal), then shows the answer.
5. **Frequency first, chunks over single words.** Order vocabulary by frequency band (core 3k, then AWL/AVL, then 5k–9k). At least 40% of new items should be collocations or chunks, each with an example sentence from the session's text.
6. **Text-linked vocabulary.** New items come from the day's reading or listening text and are pre-taught just before it. The text is chosen at **95–98% known coverage**, estimated from the learner's known-word list.
7. **Daily comprehensible input.** At least **30% of the session** is meaning-focused reading or listening. Offer an optional "extra input" extension of graded readers and podcasts, logged as input minutes.
8. **Listening = comprehension + decoding.** Each listening block runs gist, then detail, then dictation of 2–3 sentences, then the transcript, then shadowing of the same clip.
9. **Grammar cycle.** Each grammar point runs: 2–3 min explicit explanation, then structured input (interpretation), then controlled output, then free output. It is recycled at about +1, +3, +7, +21 and +60 days via FSRS-scheduled grammar items, **interleaved with its confusable partners**.
10. **Grammar pacing.** Introduce **1 new grammar point every 2–3 days** in the B1 phase, 2 per week in B2, and in the IELTS phase focus on error-driven review.
11. **Pushed output every day.** Every session includes at least one speaking or writing production task tied to the day's language. The learner records or writes first and sees a model afterwards (noticing the gap).
12. **Fluency with known material only.** Every session has one fluency block that uses *no new language*: 4/3/2 monologue with a decreasing timer, speed-reading of an easy text, or re-listening at 1.1–1.25× speed.
13. **Task repetition.** Repeat speaking tasks: the same prompt on day N, day N+2 and day N+7, and the learner compares recordings. Writing tasks require a **rewrite** after feedback.
14. **Feedback design.** Use a prompt first (a hint or rule clue). On the second miss give explicit correction (inline diff plus a one-line rule). The learner must produce the correct form before moving on. Mark at most 3 error types per output task. Keep the tone neutral and specific, without red-X shaming.
15. **Desirable, not undesirable, difficulty.** Target about 80–90% success in practice. If accuracy is above 95% for 3 days, increase the difficulty (new-item count, text level, timer). Below 75%, decrease it.
16. **Phase advancement by evidence, not by calendar alone.** Move to the next phase only when (a) a vocabulary size estimate meets the phase target, (b) review accuracy is at least 85%, and (c) the phase checkpoint test meets its threshold. Otherwise extend the phase and tell the learner why.
17. **Monthly checkpoint.** Every 4 weeks, run a 20–30 min mini-test (vocabulary size, listening, reading, and a timed write) to recalibrate text level and new-item rate.
18. **IELTS format only late.** Exam-format practice is ≤10% of time in B1, ≤20% in B2, and 40–50% in the IELTS phase. Before that, build general proficiency.
19. **Short blocks, single focus.** Blocks last 5–15 min, each with one clear goal. Alternate modality (eyes, ears, mouth, hands). After about 45 min, insert a 2-min break screen.
20. **Forgiving habit mechanics.** Use the "Start today" button, a visible daily plan length, and a streak that tolerates 1 missed day per week. On low-energy days, a "short day" mode (about 20–25 min) runs reviews, a short input block and a micro-output task. It is never zero.
21. **Visible competence.** End each session with 3 numbers: estimated known words, input minutes this week, and progress toward band 7.5. Do not use XP or leaderboards that reward speed over learning.
22. **Small, safe choices for autonomy.** Offer an optional topic preference for reading and listening, and an optional choice of speaking prompt from 2 options. Never let the learner skip reviews or production.
23. **Avoid:** passive rereading, recognition-only drills, blocked same-rule drills without later interleaving, more than 20 new words/day, decontextualised word lists without examples, marking every error, and test-only practice before the IELTS phase.
24. **Offline-first evidence.** All scheduling (FSRS), progress and checkpoint data stay local. Speaking self-assessment uses checklists and model answers so it works without a network. Online AI feedback can be an optional enhancement.

### Guided session template: B1 phase (60 min; the 90-min values are in brackets)

1. **Warm-up recall** (2): yesterday's 3 key chunks, said aloud.
2. **SRS reviews** (8 [10]): due vocabulary and grammar items, retrieval-based.
3. **Pre-teach** (6 [8]): today's 8–15 new chunks from the text, with an example sentence and audio.
4. **Grammar mini-lesson** (6 [10]): explicit rule, then structured input, then controlled output. On non-grammar days this time goes to interleaved grammar review.
5. **Reading or listening for meaning** (12 [18]): a graded text at 98% coverage, gist question, then detail questions. Target forms are highlighted.
6. **Decoding micro-listening** (5 [8]): dictation of 2–3 sentences, transcript, shadowing.
7. **Pushed output** (10 [16]): speak 1–2 min or write 80–120 words using today's chunks. Compare with a model, take prompt-then-explicit feedback, and redo once.
8. **Fluency** (8 [12]): 4/3/2 monologue on a familiar topic, or a speed read of an old easy text.
9. **Wrap-up** (3 [6]): 3 progress numbers. New errors are auto-added to SRS.

### Guided session template: B2 phase (60 [90] min)

1. **SRS reviews** (10 [12]).
2. **Pre-teach** (6 [9]): chunks and AWL items from the day's text, plus a collocation focus.
3. **Grammar or discourse focus** (4 [6]): complex structures (conditionals, passives, relative clauses, linking), interleaved with earlier points.
4. **Input** (18 [28]): a longer authentic-adapted article or podcast at 95–98% coverage, plus micro-listening on 2 sentences.
5. **Output** (14 [22]): an IELTS-style speaking Part 1–2 prompt (repeated across days) or a 150-word letter. Self-check with a band-descriptor checklist, then a rewrite.
6. **Fluency** (8 [13]): 4/3/2, or re-listen at 1.15× while shadowing.

### Guided session template: IELTS phase (60 [90] min)

1. **SRS reviews** (8 [10]): including personal error cards.
2. **Targeted language** (6 [8]): topic vocabulary (environment, work, health…) and error-driven grammar.
3. **Skill block** (16 [26]), rotating daily: GT Reading (timed section), Listening (one section), or Writing Task 1 (20 min).
4. **Output block** (22 [36]), rotating daily: Writing Task 2 (40 min on 90-min days, plan-only plus a paragraph on 60-min days) or Speaking Parts 1–3. Feedback against band descriptors, then a rewrite or re-record.
5. **Fluency** (6 [8]): a Part 2 cue card repeated 3× with a shrinking time limit.
6. **Wrap-up** (2 [2]): estimated band trend.
7. **Weekly**: one full-length practice section under exam timing, rotating through skills. In the final 3 weeks, run 1–2 full mock tests per week.
